DEFINITION:

This cluster is conceptually anchored in the application of system dynamics modeling to analyze the interrelated challenges of carbon management, energy transition, and the integration of both renewable and non-renewable power sources, specifically encompassing solar, photovoltaic, battery, and coal technologies. Methodologically, the core literature operationalizes system dynamics—primarily via platforms such as Vensim and Powersim—to model and simulate complex feedbacks among economic, technological, policy, and behavioral variables that drive resource allocation, energy consumption, power system performance, market dynamics, and emission outcomes [UT 259][UT 297][UT 300][UT 160][UT 255][UT 170][UT 165][UT 315]. The boundaries of this thematic cluster are delineated by a breadth of applications spanning urban transportation, construction, coal mining, waste-to-energy conversion, renewable energy supply chains, and microgrid management, with scenario and policy design forming key methodological pillars [UT 279][UT 381][UT 399][UT 412][UT 438]. Modeling efforts range from decomposing carbon and greenhouse gas emissions sources to evaluating mitigation strategies and decarbonization pathways across sectors, integrating the supply side (e.g., photovoltaic, solar, battery, and coal power) and demand side (e.g., household adoption of renewables, electric vehicles) [UT 117][UT 238][UT 393]. Analyses extend to cross-sectoral interactions, including the coupling of low-carbon policy interventions, distributed energy resources, and the management of existing coal assets, with an emphasis on scenario-based policy evaluation and circular economy initiatives [UT 120][UT 326]. Collectively, this cluster positions system dynamics as both a unifying methodological framework and an analytical lens for exploring the causal mechanisms, dynamic interactions, and temporal evolution of carbon, energy, power, and greenhouse gas-related phenomena in pursuit of innovation and sustainable transitions.

TRENDS:

Recent trends in system dynamics research related to carbon, energy, and power systems reveal a comprehensive shift towards more holistic, integrated, and empirically grounded modeling approaches. There is a pronounced emphasis on the combined analysis of demand- and supply-side transformations, underscored by the integration of renewable technologies—including photovoltaic (PV) generation, solar power, battery energy storage, and electric vehicles—as well as demand-side management in both residential and microgrid contexts [UT 300][UT 130][UT 73]. Scenario analysis has become a central methodological feature, enabling the evaluation of greenhouse gas and carbon emissions trajectories under alternative policy, technological, and behavioral scenarios—ranging from carbon emission trading and tradable green certificates to new energy vehicle mandates and direct financial incentives [UT 160][UT 76][UT 373][UT 255][UT 393]. The methodological frontier is marked by the use of clustering and validation techniques for scenario robustness [UT 259], and by multi-method integrations such as agent-based modeling for transport emissions [UT 315]. Empirical applications now span diverse sectors and geographical regions—including coal mining optimization, waste-to-energy valorization, and sectoral coupling between renewables, coal, and power infrastructure—with a strong focus on tailoring models to local climate, economic, and policy environments [UT 326][UT 297][UT 173][UT 260][UT 399]. The models increasingly capture cross-sectoral feedbacks (e.g., water-energy-food-carbon nexus [UT 170][UT 238]) and social dynamics, such as consumer adoption patterns and incentive effects [UT 393]. Many studies explicitly validate simulations with primary or historical data, reflecting a commitment to context-sensitive and actionable insights [UT 399][UT 412]. Collectively, this thematic family demonstrates a progression toward multi-scalar, policy-relevant, and sustainability-aligned system dynamics applications at the carbon-energy interface.

CHALLENGES:

This cluster is distinguished by persistent conceptual, methodological, and implementation challenges arising from the inherent complexity and uncertainty of modeling carbon, energy, and resource systems. Conceptually, there is difficulty in disentangling the dynamic interdependence among sectoral variables—including policy, infrastructure, market behaviors, and environmental impacts—in the context of volatile renewable output, entrenched fossil-based infrastructures such as coal, and multi-factorial emission sources like household behavior and technological change [UT 255][UT 259][UT 297][UT 130]. Methodological hurdles are apparent in the absence of comprehensive protocols for embedding critical drivers into scenario planning and system dynamics models, coupled with challenges in validating model clusters and ensuring prediction robustness, especially as boundaries expand to encompass cross-sectoral interactions such as the water–energy–food–carbon nexus and the water–energy tradeoffs in biodiesel systems [UT 170][UT 238][UT 259]. Data quality, calibration, and scenario realism pose further difficulties, with critical variables—such as climatic impacts, technology performance, and resource stockpiles for renewables and coal—often remaining under-quantified, thereby undermining the reliability of policy prescriptions [UT 399][UT 381][UT 279]. Implementation barriers frequently manifest as limited grid modernization, insufficient financial incentives, regulatory and behavioral obstacles in solar PV and battery adoption, lagging technology maturity in emerging markets, and institutional inertia resisting system-wide innovation [UT 373][UT 73][UT 297][UT 260][UT 393]. Further, managing scalability thresholds in distributed energy storage, optimizing risk management in power supply chains, and effecting real-time adjustments in power microgrids under demand fluctuations remain unresolved [UT 173][UT 160][UT 130][UT 175]. Contextual challenges—such as government policy misconfigurations, path dependency, and stakeholder capacity—add variability and impede the generalizability of insights across power, renewable, and carbon systems [UT 412][UT 438]. Collectively, these issues underscore a pressing need for more validated, integrative, and adaptable modeling approaches grounded in richer data, in order to meet the nuanced requirements of sustainable energy transitions and dynamic policy environments [UT 279][UT 120][UT 326].

OPPORTUNITIES:

In this cluster, significant research opportunities arise from synthesizing diverse gaps and emerging perspectives identified across the literature, particularly with respect to carbon, energy, and power system transitions. A key avenue involves the development of multi-dimensional and adaptive system dynamics models that integrate advanced scenario planning to enhance model representativeness, predictive power, and empirical calibration across complex nexus contexts—such as water-energy-food-carbon interrelations and the interplay of renewable and coal resources [UT 259][UT 297][UT 170][UT 238]. Future studies should expand the systematic identification, validation, and granularity of variables, especially for tracking carbon flows, technology adoption, and their socioeconomic drivers, while addressing double-counted or underperforming renewables within legacy infrastructure [UT 279][UT 399][UT 255]. The resilience and scalability of distributed energy storage, the optimization of maintenance strategies, and the integration of sectoral coupling—including green hydrogen within renewable-coal frameworks—represent vital, yet underexplored, research frontiers for boosting both operational performance and emissions reductions [UT 173][UT 297]. The literature also highlights the need to optimize policy instruments such as tradable green certificates and emissions trading schemes for dynamic market and technology scenarios, while contextually enriching energy transition models through behavioral, financial, and regulatory factors, especially in emerging economies [UT 160][UT 373][UT 260][UT 393][UT 315]. Synthesis across studies further underscores the value of hybrid modeling and real-time, participatory approaches for strengthening longitudinal validation, risk management in sustainable power chains, and adaptive policy, as well as for leveraging richer data streams and stakeholder engagement to close persistent policy-practice gaps [UT 326][UT 412][UT 438]. Comparative, cross-industry research and deeper exploration of the circular economy, gendered employment, and supply chain implications—exemplified in cases such as jute stick charcoal—will further support robust, equitable, and climate-aligned energy transition strategies [UT 438].

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster within system dynamics, the literature converges on a set of recommendations aimed at bridging the gap between advanced modeling and actionable outcomes for carbon, energy, and power systems, with a focus on renewable and coal-related transitions. System dynamics models should be advanced as integrated decision-support tools that combine the simulation of technical, economic, and policy dynamics with real-time data analytics, particularly for responsive energy management in microgrids, battery systems, and photovoltaic or solar infrastructures [UT 130][UT 173][UT 300]. Expanding participatory modeling—via interviews, workshops, and stakeholder-driven scenario design—ensures that models address local knowledge, grid modernization challenges, social acceptance, and market readiness, thus enabling regionally tailored pathways and reinforcing buy-in from policy makers, industry, and communities [UT 73][UT 373][UT 381][UT 438][UT 259][UT 160][UT 76]. Model architectures should support dynamic flexibility and adaptive policy design, allowing timely adjustment to evolving market, technological, and environmental conditions to promote steady progress toward decarbonization and low-carbon energy goals [UT 160][UT 297]. Integration of high-resolution empirical data—especially from underrepresented regions or sectors, such as developing countries’ transportation fleets or coal value chains—can improve credibility and robustness by enhancing context-specific feedbacks and validation [UT 255][UT 170][UT 393]. Systematic use of multi-criteria decision analysis, coupled with transparent reporting of limitations, model validation, sensitivity testing, and explicit communication of co-benefits like job creation or waste reduction, will strengthen the alignment of models with sustainability indices and Sustainable Development Goals [UT 165][UT 326][UT 438][UT 381]. Finally, dissemination through user-friendly interfaces—such as business intelligence dashboards and regular reporting for operational and legislative audiences—will accelerate the real-world uptake of system dynamics insights in power, coal, and renewable energy domains [UT 260][UT 259][UT 412][UT 399][UT 279][UT 175][UT 393].
