DEFINITION:

This thematic cluster delineates a research area in which system dynamics serves as the methodological and conceptual foundation for analyzing, simulating, and optimizing complex health-related processes, integrating topics such as public health policy, epidemiology, disease prevalence, prevention, cost analysis, vaccination strategies, and the environmental-health interface—including issues like landfill management and coronavirus modeling. Methodologically, studies in this cluster distinguish themselves through the integration of qualitative and quantitative data, employing simulation, feedback loop analysis, causal loop diagrams (CLDs), stock-flow models, scenario simulation, structural analysis, and the calibration of models with real-world data [UT 403][UT 140][UT 65][UT 462][UT 301][UT 429][UT 370][UT 13][UT 270][UT 125][UT 200][UT 236][UT 291][UT 344][UT 345][UT 351][UT 438]. Core to these approaches is the engagement with multi-level factors spanning social determinants of health, organizational behaviors, disease transmission pathways, healthcare service utilization, resource allocation, cost-effectiveness, and environmental determinants—allowing multi-dimensional modeling of health outcomes, disease burden, and intervention coverage. Dynamic modeling platforms, such as Vensim, support scenario analysis and stakeholder engagement to evaluate the effectiveness of various policy levers, including vaccination campaigns, preventive interventions, food and resource distribution, waste management, and pharmaceutical pricing. The cluster spans both infectious and chronic diseases (e.g., TB, cancer, COVID-19) as well as cross-sectoral topics where environmental factors, such as landfill practices, are intricately connected to public health outcomes. Central across these studies is the explicit modeling of dynamic interactions, causal relationships, feedback loops, and system-wide drivers, which together enable a nuanced understanding of health system complexity and support the prediction and optimization of population health and cost trajectories under diverse intervention scenarios.

TRENDS:

Recent trends in this thematic cluster demonstrate both diversification and methodological maturation in the application of system dynamics to health and epidemiology. Historically rooted in infectious disease models, current research now encompasses a broader range of health domains, including hospital resource forecasting, clinical innovations, health service optimization, and the prevention and management of communicable and non-communicable diseases [UT 403][UT 462][UT 429][UT 301][UT 370]. There is a growing emphasis on integrating age-stratified, socioeconomic, and patient-centric factors into models—particularly in pandemic vaccine allocation and the assessment of intervention effectiveness—while also addressing the prevalence and cost of disease within specific populations [UT 200][UT 345][UT 236][UT 291][UT 344]. Methodologically, advances are underscored by the persistent use of Causal Loop Diagrams (CLDs) and stock-flow models, now more frequently combined with empirical data, sensitivity analyses, and rigorous scenario simulation to test real-world policy and prevention strategies [UT 140][UT 65][UT 462]. The empirical scope extends beyond hospital and clinic settings to public health policy, social determinants of health, and environmental health governance—exemplified by studies modeling the intersection of food policy, environmental shocks, and landfill avoidance [UT 13][UT 125][UT 429][UT 370][UT 438]. Notably, the response to emergent health crises such as coronavirus has driven improvements in model calibration and validation across diverse contexts, ensuring results are transferable and actionable [UT 351]. This evolution in system dynamics research signals a shift toward more holistic, context-sensitive, and policy-oriented approaches, supporting sustainable improvements at both organizational and community levels while systematically addressing resource, cost, and epidemiological complexities.

CHALLENGES:

System dynamics modeling in health, epidemiology, and public health interventions is confronted by a complex array of conceptual, methodological, and practical challenges that persist despite ongoing research. Conceptually, representing the multi-factorial determinants of health—including behavioral, environmental, psychosocial, and biomedical factors—within unified and adaptable dynamic models remains a significant obstacle, particularly as models must respond to emerging diseases such as coronavirus and varied health system contexts [UT 345][UT 236][UT 140]. Methodologically, the calibration and validation of these models are often hindered by limited, incomplete, or non-transferable datasets, uncertainty in parameterization, and the challenge of integrating disparate qualitative and quantitative information—a situation further complicated by the necessity to accurately simulate dynamic interactions, adaptive behaviors, feedback loop complexity, and delayed effects within and across system components [UT 140][UT 65][UT 351][UT 344][UT 462]. In epidemiological contexts, the prevalence of transmission heterogeneity, regional interdependencies, and persistent external risks such as COVID-19 exacerbate difficulties in predictive accuracy and hinder effective prevention strategies [UT 270][UT 301]. From an implementation perspective, institutional inertia, stakeholder resistance, infrastructural constraints—such as those affecting optimal landfill management or broader waste-to-resource initiatives—and ethical tensions in vaccine allocation or health cost containment pose significant barriers to translating system dynamics insights into actionable policies [UT 403][UT 370][UT 438][UT 200][UT 291]. Moreover, the translation of simulated environments to real-world health, prevention, and vaccination outcomes is limited by the inability to capture nuanced patient, community, or policy dynamics, underscoring the need for sustained empirical grounding, advanced sensitivity analyses, and formal validation to build stakeholder trust and ensure robust scenario planning [UT 140][UT 65][UT 462].

OPPORTUNITIES:

Significant research opportunities within this cluster arise from the convergence of gaps in policy integration, empirical substantiation, and stakeholder engagement, as well as from underexplored intersections highlighted in the current literature. Advancing synergistic, double-duty policy frameworks—moving beyond isolated approaches to either undernutrition or overnutrition—represents a crucial direction for addressing the double burden of malnutrition and related health inequities, particularly in low and middle-income settings [UT 462]. Future inquiry should prioritize the deeper integration of behavioral, social, and environmental determinants into models of infectious disease and chronic conditions, thereby refining prevention strategies to be both biomedical and contextually grounded [UT 345][UT 236]. The exploitation of real-world evidence and routinely collected data, along with the incorporation of real-time data streams, can enhance simulation accuracy, dynamic updating, and evidence-based decision support—especially relevant for optimizing drug pricing, value-based care, and preventive programs [UT 13][UT 301][UT 344][UT 351]. Cross-sectoral modeling of feedbacks between health, environment, and social policy—including direct analysis of landfill reduction, circular economy initiatives, and their impacts on community health outcomes—offers fertile ground for system-wide interventions [UT 429][UT 370][UT 438]. Additional attention to model-based optimization for emerging health technologies, such as genomics in cancer care, should examine reimbursement, ethical, and access issues within dynamic health system contexts [UT 291][UT 403]. Bridging methodological divides through comprehensive mixed methods, iterative scenario testing, foresight analysis, and participatory stakeholder engagement at all levels promises to yield models that are more accurate, sustainable, and locally relevant [UT 65][UT 140]. Finally, the field would benefit from enhanced methodological standardization for model validation and calibration, which would improve transferability, accelerate policy uptake, and support more robust anticipatory governance in public health systems.

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster, future research should emphasize sustained, participatory model co-design and iterative validation involving policymakers, clinical practitioners, community members, and local implementers. Such engagement ensures that system dynamics models are context-relevant, actionable, and tailored to the complexities of real-world health, epidemiology, and prevention challenges [UT 429][UT 370][UT 200][UT 345][UT 236]. Strengthening multisector partnerships—including actors from health, agriculture, waste management, and social welfare—can facilitate resilient, integrated interventions and enable rapid, coordinated responses to emergent shocks, as highlighted in food rescue and supply chain policy simulations [UT 429][UT 370][UT 438]. For intervention prioritization, translating models into policy should foreground strategies that reduce health inequalities by integrating targeted health education, behavioral and psychosocial factors, and locally tailored epidemiological insights, thereby ensuring equitable vaccine and prevention allocation [UT 403][UT 462][UT 200][UT 345][UT 236]. Embedding rigorous real-world data, adaptive learning mechanisms, and ongoing data integration into models—across macro health system and cost estimation applications—enhances usability, relevance, and dynamic scenario planning for ministries of health and operational managers, particularly in the context of prevalence monitoring, emerging threats like coronavirus, and resource allocation [UT 65][UT 301][UT 140][UT 291][UT 344][UT 351]. Multi-country calibration and scenario adaptability should become standard to support crisis preparedness and maximize trust in epidemiological modeling [UT 351]. Finally, standardized transparent workflows for validation, documentation, and participatory review, along with tailored dissemination strategies and continuous stakeholder engagement, are recommended to increase model interpretability, credibility, and uptake, ensuring that environmental impact, landfill reduction, cost, and health outcomes directly inform effective decision-making and sustainable health policy gains [UT 125][UT 13][UT 429][UT 438].
