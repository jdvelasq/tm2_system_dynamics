DEFINITION:

This thematic cluster is defined by its integrative focus on system dynamics applications addressing carbon reduction, technological innovation, green transformation, and market responses across multiple sectors—including construction, urban transportation, manufacturing, and energy systems. Fundamentally, it relies on methodological frameworks developed in system dynamics modeling, most frequently realized through platforms such as Vensim or Powersim, to map, simulate, and forecast the multifactor interactions among carbon emissions, energy consumption, prefabricated building technologies, regulatory market instruments, and policy interventions (including emissions trading, subsidies, and green certificates) [UT 426, UT 136, UT 395, UT 73, UT 125, UT 76, UT 260, UT 160, UT 316, UT 429; UT 222, UT 255, UT 418, UT 50, UT 170, UT 407, UT 27, UT 31, UT 165, UT 315; UT 279, UT 288, UT 374, UT 381, UT 458]. The operational boundaries of this cluster extend from micro-level analyses—such as enterprise transformation for zero-carbon construction, safety incentives in prefabrication, and passenger mobility—to macro-level processes including sector-wide electrification, global energy transition, and market-level emission robustness. System dynamics is employed to structure endogenous and exogenous drivers in theoretical, technical, and policy-oriented scenarios, elucidating feedback loops and stock-flow relations that shape the robustness and adaptability of low-carbon and green initiatives. By systematically incorporating stakeholder behavior, managerial and financial subsystems, and the circulation dynamics of green and energy markets, this research family treats decarbonization and sustainable transformation as emergent, multi-scalar processes—where the leverage points for effective intervention are dynamically explored and evaluated. Collectively, the keywords carbon, technological, prefabricated, energy, passenger, construction, subsidy, green, market, emission, robustness, and zero-carbon are naturally embedded as both empirical focal points and analytical constructs within this systems-oriented approach to understanding and managing sustainable change.

TRENDS:

Recent trends within this thematic family of system dynamics research demonstrate a decisive movement toward holistic, integrated modeling approaches that capture the complex interplay among carbon management, technological advancement, prefabricated construction, energy systems, market mechanisms, and policy interventions. Across recent literature, there is robust evidence of model sophistication, exemplified by the incorporation of multiple feedback subsystems—spanning policy, technology, organizational behavior, and market structures—and the routine application of scenario-based planning to simulate emission trajectories and green transitions in domains such as low-carbon manufacturing, the prefabricated building sector, renewable energy deployment, and sustainable passenger transportation [UT 426, UT 136, UT 160, UT 73, UT 260]. The prevalence of policy simulation—encompassing government subsidies, market-based emission trading, and green certificates—highlights broader exploration of regulatory instruments for zero-carbon and global sustainability objectives [UT 125, UT 76, UT 136]. Empirical rigor is further enhanced through integration with complementary analytical methods, like DEMATEL and co-occurrence network analysis, facilitating robust causal mapping and variable selection [UT 374, UT 288]. Application diversity has expanded to encompass urban and rural locales, region-specific systems (e.g., urban U.S., China, Jordan, New Zealand), and sectoral foci including heavy industry, food redistribution, and risk assessment for emerging energy sectors [UT 316, UT 395, UT 429, UT 279, UT 381, UT 458, UT 334]. Multi-scenario and sensitivity analyses are leveraged to test the robustness of interventions—ranging from innovation in technology and prefabricated methods to subsidy design and market incentives—thereby supporting nuanced policy guidance and strategic planning. Ultimately, the field’s methodological trajectory indicates increasing granularity, internationalization, and interdisciplinary depth, cementing system dynamics as an essential tool for addressing the multifaceted challenge of sustainable, zero-carbon transitions across global construction, energy, and market systems [UT 27, UT 222, UT 31, UT 418, UT 407, UT 170, UT 315, UT 255, UT 165].

CHALLENGES:

This thematic cluster is characterized by persistent conceptual, methodological, and implementation challenges that collectively hinder the advancement and application of system dynamics approaches in domains such as carbon reduction, technological adoption, prefabricated construction, energy management, and green transformation. Conceptually, capturing the full complexity of multi-level systems—including feedback mechanisms, non-linearities, and overlapping influences of economic, technological, behavioral, and policy variables—remains elusive, often leading to ambiguity in defining actionable sector-specific pathways (e.g., for carbon or green value indices) and complicating the attribution of intervention outcomes [UT 426, UT 316, UT 160, UT 418, UT 170, UT 374, UT 381]. Path dependency and a lack of endogenous drive in high-impact sectors, along with resistance to change rooted in social norms or legacy objectives, further undermine the sustainability of transitions and obscure effective leverage points [UT 136, UT 418, UT 407, UT 395]. Methodologically, the reliability and robustness of system dynamics models are constrained by limited data sufficiency, sample sizes, and parameter calibration, as well as challenges in modeling actor heterogeneity and ensuring empirical validation beyond scenario-based sensitivity analysis [UT 395, UT 255, UT 31, UT 407, UT 381, UT 334, UT 351, UT 374]. The integration of hybrid methodological frameworks (e.g., SD-GIS, DEMATEL), cross-sectoral modeling (linking energy–water–food–carbon nexuses), and the communication of systematic insights further demand careful harmonization and present ongoing uncertainties [UT 170, UT 27]. Implementation barriers—ranging from low policy coordination, insufficient market and technological capacity, data limitations, and limited stakeholder awareness to variable impacts of subsidies and policy interventions—continue to restrict the generalizability and scalability of insights, particularly under context-dependent regulatory and managerial landscapes [UT 429, UT 160, UT 125, UT 260, UT 76, UT 316, UT 315, UT 50, UT 288, UT 458]. Collectively, these obstacles hamper efforts to operationalize, validate, and scale system dynamics-informed solutions for zero-carbon, robust, and globally relevant sustainability transitions.

OPPORTUNITIES:

This thematic cluster highlights several research opportunities emerging from unresolved conceptual and methodological challenges. A pressing avenue lies in broadening system dynamics models to more comprehensively integrate behavioral, psychological, and infrastructural variables such as ICT infrastructure, digital twin architectures, technology maturity, and consumer/developer willingness—especially within electrified transportation, smart construction, and prefabricated networks [UT 260, UT 426, UT 27, UT 288]. Advancing hybrid, multi-method approaches—combining system dynamics with agent-based modeling, network analysis, DEMATEL, GIS-linkages, and scenario planning—can capture sectoral heterogeneity, multi-scale causal influence, and behaviorally complex agents while addressing robustness and uncertainty in zero-carbon and green transition contexts [UT 315, UT 255, UT 288, UT 374, UT 334]. There is a clear need to develop dynamic feedback frameworks that model both endogenous and exogenous drivers—including organizational efficiency, innovation rates, market shocks, land-use efficacy, and government interventions—to support robust rural-urban coordination, energy autonomy, and resilience planning [UT 316]. Empirical exploration of policy synergies and trade-offs—such as structured tests of subsidies, bundled interventions, market trading, regulatory standards, and supervisory mechanisms under varying economic and climatic shocks—will deepen longitudinal understanding of policy interaction effects and carbon emission management [UT 160, UT 76, UT 136, UT 31, UT 407, UT 381, UT 355]. Expanding multi-criteria lifecycle, risk, and robustness analyses, particularly for technological upgrades and production processes, can improve the predictive value of interventions and support globally scalable low-carbon and market-based emission strategies [UT 73, UT 125, UT 50, UT 165, UT 351]. Finally, cross-sectoral transferability—including pathways from construction to energy vehicles and lessons from food waste or manufacturing—together with comparative validation across diverse institutional and geographical settings, remains under-explored but essential for strengthening generalizability and accelerating the global green transition [UT 429, UT 395, UT 418, UT 170, UT 334].

VALUE:

To maximize the practical value of this thematic cluster, a multifaceted set of strategies is essential for enhancing the effectiveness, robustness, and scalability of system dynamics applications in the pursuit of global carbon reduction, green transformation, and sustainability goals. First, early and sustained collaboration among modelers, policymakers, industry practitioners, and frontline managers is critical to ensure models are grounded in operational realities and reflect sector-specific contexts, including construction, energy, transportation, and prefabricated manufacturing [UT 407][UT 31][UT 288]. Stakeholder engagement should encompass the co-design of interventions—such as targeted subsidies, technology upgrades, or bundled incentives—and systematic feedback incorporation to improve policy accuracy and buy-in [UT 288][UT 395][UT 429]. The actionable translation of simulation findings must be prioritized by complementing scenario outputs with clear guidance on implementation timelines, resource requirements, and sectoral adaptation pathways [UT 418][UT 255][UT 279][UT 381]. Enhancing model accessibility and usability—by disseminating validated, user-friendly tools (e.g., Vensim, Powersim, digital twins, dashboards) and modular frameworks—will facilitate adoption among users with diverse technical backgrounds and support methodological transparency [UT 125][UT 160][UT 27][UT 260][UT 316]. Embedding continuous feedback mechanisms, real-time data integration, and adaptive scenario recalibration shifts decision support from static to dynamic, ensuring sustained relevance as market, technological, or regulatory landscapes evolve [UT 355][UT 458][UT 76]. Institutionalizing training and capacity building, especially in decarbonization strategies and green management, will further enhance intervention uptake across sectors [UT 50]. Finally, establishing collaborative, cross-sector platforms—leveraging SD-GIS or digital transaction markets—can amplify knowledge sharing, consolidate best practices, and accelerate on-the-ground progress toward zero-carbon and emission reduction targets globally [UT 355][UT 334][UT 165][UT 170][UT 315].
