DEFINITION:

This thematic cluster is defined by the integration of system dynamics approaches to address the interdependent challenges associated with waste generation, recycling practices, environmental management, energy consumption, landfill use, and public health—especially in rapidly urbanizing regions such as Shanghai. Methodologically, the cluster is centered on the application of system dynamics modeling, utilizing platforms such as Vensim, Stella, Powersim, and their variants to capture the causal feedbacks, dynamic behaviors, and scenario outcomes among key variables in complex socio-environmental systems [UT 72][UT 109][UT 998][UT 624][UT 860][UT 584][UT 952][UT 610][UT 501][UT 894][UT 838][UT 713][UT 836][UT 628][UT 630][UT 844][UT 864][UT 728][UT 591][UT 977][UT 549][UT 815][UT 675][UT 885][UT 875]. Empirical and simulation-based analyses extend across multiple scales, from the forecasting and management of municipal solid, construction, demolition, and electronic waste to the evaluation of battery reuse and public health responses linked to disease transmission from inadequate waste and landfill management. The cluster consistently adopts a cross-sectoral perspective, incorporating economic, social, governance, and technological dimensions of urban sustainability, and evaluates trade-offs between energy efficiency, recycling, environmental externalities, and public health outcomes. Studies frequently address the influence of policy interventions, stakeholder engagement, landfill capacity, and technological innovation, employing feedback-sensitive models to design and assess operational strategies for waste reduction and resource recovery. The systems modeled are often composite, comprising flows of materials, information, and energy, as well as embedded public health and infrastructure planning processes, thereby facilitating holistic evaluations of long-term environmental, economic, and social impacts, and revealing the regulatory leverage points critical for sustainable urban development and effective disease prevention.

TRENDS:

Recent trends in this thematic cluster reflect a distinct and accelerating emphasis on scenario-based simulation to evaluate environmental, economic, and public health outcomes of waste, recycling, and landfill management, often contrasting business-as-usual, regulatory, and circular economy approaches. System dynamics has emerged as the dominant methodology for capturing dynamic feedbacks in complex urban, peri-urban, and regional systems, enabling the examination of diverse contexts—including construction waste in Shanghai, e-waste in India, hospital waste in Tabriz, and biomass energy initiatives—across studies spanning China, Greece, Indonesia, and beyond [UT 72][UT 109][UT 831][UT 998][UT 624][UT 876][UT 860][UT 584][UT 610][UT 844][UT 838][UT 894][UT 501][UT 836][UT 864][UT 591][UT 728][UT 779][UT 854][UT 890][UT 782][UT 977][UT 549][UT 952]. The methodological trajectory reveals a progression from analyzing traditional waste streams (e.g., municipal solid waste, construction and demolition) to technologically driven domains such as electronic waste, new energy vehicle batteries, and industrial material flows. Increasing sophistication is evident in the integration of multi-scenario modeling—incorporating government subsidies, market dynamics, industry cooperation, and technological innovation—alongside robust validation protocols and causal-loop diagrams [UT 885][UT 675][UT 875][UT 815]. Empirical applications are further characterized by benchmarking local strategies against international best practices and by expanding model boundaries to link waste management decisions with broader sustainability outcomes, including greenhouse gas mitigation, energy optimization, and public health (e.g., infectious disease control, health system resilience) [UT 713][UT 836][UT 501][UT 854][UT 890][UT 782]. Collectively, this cluster evidences both the popularity of system dynamics in waste and environmental management research and an increasing drive toward holistic, transdisciplinary frameworks that integrate energy, environmental, and public policy considerations.

CHALLENGES:

This thematic cluster exposes several persistent and interrelated challenges—conceptual, methodological, and implementation-related—that constrain the full potential of system dynamics applications in waste, recycle, environmental, landfill, electronic waste, energy, construction, and public health systems across diverse contexts, such as shanghai and beyond. Conceptually, the inherent complexity of modeling multifaceted systems—where feedback loops, sectoral interdependencies, public engagement, and behavioral factors all interact dynamically—presents significant obstacles, especially when defining appropriate system boundaries and expanding them to encompass external drivers and feedbacks [UT 836][UT 838][UT 549][UT 977][UT 885][UT 875]. Methodologically, studies frequently struggle with the acquisition and integration of reliable data from disparate, often informal, sources, including official reports, enterprise records, and interviews. Data deficits related to emissions, economic losses, illegal dumping, or disease transmission not only complicate model calibration and validation but also limit the generalizability of results across regions and policy environments [UT 72][UT 624][UT 501][UT 894][UT 628][UT 864][UT 728][UT 591][UT 675]. Imperfect industry chains, heterogeneity in stakeholder behavior, and the intricacies of informal sector activity—especially in electronic and e-waste management—further undermine the accuracy and policy relevance of simulated outcomes [UT 109][UT 885][UT 860]. Implementation-wise, gaps between simulated optimal strategies (e.g., high recycling rates, widespread public participation, or disease control interventions) and real-world practice are exacerbated by regulatory inertia, inefficient collaboration, cost and technology adoption barriers, stakeholder commitment deficits, cultural biases against recycled materials, as well as public concerns about information security in electronic waste management [UT 952][UT 610][UT 838][UT 109][UT 860][UT 779][UT 728][UT 815]. Moreover, overreliance on technical modeling or big data approaches risks neglecting critical process reengineering and sociotechnical dynamics necessary for sustainable change [UT 815][UT 844][UT 630]. As a result, translating dynamic model insights into actionable policy remains an unresolved issue, complicated by local context differences in compliance, welfare infrastructure, and the ongoing evolution of environmental, construction, and energy systems [UT 998][UT 854][UT 584].

OPPORTUNITIES:

In this thematic cluster, substantive research opportunities arise from the need to bridge persistent gaps between system dynamics modeling and real-world implementation across waste management, recycling, environmental health, and urban sustainability. Promising directions include developing collaborative, closed-loop supply chains for electronic and battery waste, leveraging both domestic and international best practices to enhance resource recovery and commercial viability [UT 876][UT 831]. Expanding scenario analysis to incorporate behavioral, institutional, and socioeconomic variables—such as public attitudes toward recycled products, enterprise participation in multi-party cooperation, and the dynamic interplay between formal and informal sectors—remains crucial for increasing the predictive realism of models in contexts like Shanghai and other rapidly urbanizing regions [UT 109][UT 860][UT 501][UT 836][UT 838][UT 844]. There is also an evident need for cross-sectoral modeling that holistically integrates environmental, economic, energy, and public health outcomes, including the feedback loops between waste flows, disease transmission, air pollution, and healthcare provisioning [UT 682][UT 584][UT 628][UT 894][UT 713]. Methodologically, advances in real-time data integration, participatory stakeholder engagement, and the coupling of system dynamics with agent-based or spatial simulation models can address non-linearities, improve adaptability, and capture emergent behaviors [UT 531][UT 549][UT 610][UT 630][UT 815]. Further, underexplored areas—such as synthesizing regulatory and economic incentive impacts across multiple policy levels, systematically assessing ecological and environmental co-effects, and modeling the long-term public health consequences of electronic waste and toxic substance flows—offer rich potential for innovation [UT 779][UT 728][UT 854][UT 977][UT 885]. Finally, enhancing model robustness through hybrid and multi-model approaches, participatory process reengineering, and scenario-driven policy guidance will be pivotal in driving actionable governance and promoting evidence-based public and environmental health outcomes [UT 675][UT 875][UT 885].

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster’s research in system dynamics, a multifaceted approach should be pursued that integrates regulatory, economic, and informational interventions with model-based insights to drive optimal system performance in waste, recycle, and environmental management contexts [UT 109][UT 876]. Central to this enhancement is the incorporation of empirical scenario testing alongside robust stakeholder engagement, beginning early in the model development process and involving local governments, health authorities, industry, and community organizations to ensure relevance, feasibility, and collaborative ownership [UT 610][UT 838][UT 630][UT 591]. Embedding system dynamics models within decision-support systems and aligning model outputs with actionable metrics—such as reduction targets, cost-benefit analyses, and regulatory thresholds—will enable policymakers and managers to evaluate, communicate, and adapt their strategies across domains like landfill management, disease control, energy adoption, and environmental/public health safeguarding [UT 998][UT 624][UT 864][UT 779][UT 728][UT 885][UT 875]. Comparative benchmarking, particularly referencing best practices from regions like Shanghai, Hong Kong, and Singapore, supports knowledge transfer and local adaptation in environmental and recycling policy design [UT 728]. Investments in monitoring infrastructure, participatory reporting platforms, and real-time data integration will allow for ongoing model calibration, increased reliability, and adaptability in the face of technological, demographic, or regulatory shifts [UT 501][UT 610][UT 675]. Emphasizing interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary collaboration will link urban planning, industrial practices, public health, and environmental management for maximized social and economic returns [UT 72][UT 682][UT 584][UT 952][UT 894][UT 628]. Finally, transparency in model validation, the use of interactive visualization or communication tools, and regular updating of model parameters will further enhance the interpretability, adoption, and sustained relevance of system dynamics-based recommendations for waste reduction, recycling, and environmental and disease management [UT 728][UT 890][UT 815][UT 836][UT 844].
