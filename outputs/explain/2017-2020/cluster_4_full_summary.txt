DEFINITION:

In this thematic cluster, system dynamics is consistently defined and operationalized as the principal methodological framework for modeling, simulating, and optimizing complex supply chain systems, with an emphasis on inventory, supply, manufacturer, and supplier interactions. The literature typically employs quantitative dynamic modeling approaches using specialized simulation tools—including Vensim, Powersim Studio, Stella Architect, iThink, and AnyLogic—to capture the structural complexity and feedback-driven behavior of multi-tier supply chains spanning industrial, agro-industrial, and manufacturing contexts. Studies explicitly construct models that represent a range of actors such as manufacturers, suppliers, logistics providers, and other key stakeholders, thereby reflecting the intricate coordination mechanisms and decision interfaces underlying supply chain operations. The scope of research encompasses diverse supply chain architectures, including closed-loop and open configurations, multi-supplier–multi-manufacturer systems, collaborative pooling arrangements, and sector-specific settings such as perishable and energy-oriented supply chains. Methodological attention is placed on inventory control strategies (e.g., CPFR, VMI, JMI), risk management in manufacturing, capacity planning, pricing, logistics performance evaluation, and collaborative practices. Central to this cluster is the dynamic interplay among supply structure, inventory fluctuations, manufacturer processes, and supplier relationships, as well as the integration of policy scenarios, resource recovery, sustainability measures, and uncertainty management. Simulation-driven analyses routinely address operational contingencies—such as emergency replenishments and cross-chain collaborations—enabling a holistic, feedback-based understanding of supply chain effectiveness, adaptability, and resource availability. Ultimately, the cluster is unified by its focus on leveraging system dynamics for robust, quantitative decision support, proactive risk mitigation, and optimization of real-world supply and inventory systems [UT 876][UT 500][UT 534][UT 707][UT 780][UT 826][UT 621][UT 795][UT 547][UT 869][UT 609][UT 833][UT 942][UT 936][UT 778][UT 885].

TRENDS:

This thematic cluster reveals a pronounced and evolving trend in system dynamics research focused on supply networks, with particular attention to the integration of hybrid modeling approaches—such as quantitative stock-flow diagrams, causal loop mapping, and expert-driven soft system elements—to reflect the complex interactions among manufacturers, suppliers, and inventory systems [UT 876][UT 707]. Recent contributions highlight a notable expansion in the empirical application of dynamic and simulation-based methods, including the use of Vensim, to evaluate supply chain performance, risk, policy, and coordination mechanisms across diverse sectors such as new energy vehicle batteries, agro-industrial products, perishable goods, MSMEs, and biofuels [UT 795][UT 833][UT 869][UT 942]. The literature demonstrates a diversification from traditional production-inventory problems in sectors like cement and lighting fixtures toward innovative inventory management paradigms (e.g., CPFR, VMI, JMI, pooling systems), recovery and eco-design initiatives for sustainability, and systems designed to mitigate volatility, such as the bullwhip effect through fuzzy logic integrations [UT 500][UT 119][UT 621][UT 534]. Cross-chain and multi-level collaboration models have become increasingly prevalent, shifting research emphasis from single-chain optimization to holistic, interconnected supply network perspectives involving collaborative mechanisms, resource coordination, and alignment between authorities and enterprises for objectives such as food safety and service delivery [UT 547][UT 936][UT 609][UT 138][UT 684]. Methodological advancements are complemented by the broader adoption of user-interactive interfaces, model-driven decision support systems, and empirical sensitivity analysis for managerial and policy-oriented decision-making, particularly in the context of risk quantification, public policy formation, and value-oriented resource handling [UT 826][UT 833][UT 885][UT 778]. Collectively, these trends underscore the central role of system dynamics in advancing both the conceptual understanding and practical management of contemporary supply, manufacturer, and inventory systems.

CHALLENGES:

This thematic cluster reveals a persistent set of conceptual and methodological challenges that impede progress in the system dynamics of supply and inventory management. At the conceptual level, capturing the inherent complexity, dynamic feedback loops, and behavioral uncertainties embedded within multi-tiered supply systems remains a formidable obstacle, particularly as uncertainty, policy variation, and risk are integrated into model structures [UT 534][UT 771][UT 942]. Notably, traditional demand forecasting often fails to mitigate phenomena such as the bullwhip effect, given the unpredictable and vague nature of supply chains—prompting a shift toward hybrid frameworks employing fuzzy logic and expert-based linguistic parameters, albeit at the cost of increased model complexity [UT 534]. Methodologically, maintaining construct and behavioral validity within system dynamics models is challenged by gaps between simulation constructs and real-world operational correlations, as well as pervasive shortages of reliable, granular data at each stage of the supply network needed for scenario analysis and robust policy validation [UT 826][UT 771][UT 942]. Implementation-wise, issues such as low interoperability in enterprise resource planning, logistics information inconsistency arising from human errors, delayed deliveries, and communication gaps between designers and manufacturers amplify inventory and supply risks [UT 942][UT 936]. Collaborative supply and inventory models are further hampered by the difficulty of calibrating joint strategies in co-opetitive contexts and extending analytical reach to cross-chain or multi-level industrial clusters, which demand nuanced coordination among heterogeneous actors [UT 547][UT 609][UT 795][UT 138]. Transforming simulation insights into concrete operational improvements remains nontrivial, as inconsistent market policies, fluctuating supply capacities for perishable goods, persistent pricing distortions, and the unpredictability of high-volatility events such as seasonal demand or emergent e-waste flows constrain systematic implementation [UT 833][UT 869][UT 885]. The integration of sustainability, environmental, and social criteria further complicates calibration and verification, necessitating hybridized data sources, multi-stakeholder alignment, and advanced validation strategies [UT 707][UT 621].

OPPORTUNITIES:

Significant research opportunities remain in advancing the conceptual and methodological foundations of supply chain system dynamics, particularly in developing robust, scalable, and interoperable dynamic models that tightly integrate behavioral, environmental, and policy-driven components [UT 138][UT 684]. Expanding these models to support system-level coordination by formalizing interaction protocols among diverse stakeholders—including suppliers, regulators, manufacturers, and logistics providers—remains a pressing need, especially within cross-chain and multi-echelon structures [UT 138][UT 684]. Enhancing the integration of dynamic models with decision support systems to facilitate real-time scenario adjustments can bridge persistent gaps in quantitative information and adaptive management, particularly under volatile demand and uncertainty [UT 826][UT 500]. Addressing low interoperability among information systems and the persistent gaps between design and manufacturing warrants research into digitally integrated, adaptive supply architectures that enable real-time information exchange and cross-organizational response [UT 942]. Incorporating sustainability and recoverability metrics—by coupling generic recoverability indices with resource flow simulations—presents compelling avenues for advancing eco-innovation in product, inventory, and supply chain design [UT 621][UT 885]. Further, hybridization between soft and hard systems methods, including the combination of expert opinion with dynamic simulations for nuanced performance evaluation and scenario planning, is underexplored, with promising results demonstrated in niche sectors such as the MSME agro-industry [UT 707]. The development of hybrid fuzzy logic and system dynamics models offers significant potential to more accurately capture uncertainty, human decision-making, and policy effects within supply chains [UT 534]. Additionally, more granular and time-dependent modeling approaches are needed to support resilience in supply and inventory management—especially for perishable goods or renewable energy contexts marked by high volatility or seasonality [UT 869][UT 833]. Finally, the extension of system dynamics to multi-agent, globalized supply networks—including the modeling of network effects, digital marketplace phenomena, value-oriented management, and emergency inventory coordination—remains a fertile direction for addressing disruptions and optimizing both supplier and manufacturer incentives [UT 609][UT 778][UT 547].

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster within the core area of system dynamics, it is essential to prioritize the development of user-friendly, actionable interfaces and model-driven policy experimentation, enabling practitioners and decision-makers to dynamically adapt supply, inventory, and manufacturer strategies to real-world conditions [UT 826][UT 500][UT 795][UT 869][UT 936]. Increasing the fidelity and accessibility of system dynamics models—through modular design, scenario-based templates, and plug-and-play modules that accommodate sector-specific calibration—will support broader usability and effective iterative scenario analysis [UT 826][UT 795]. Early warning signals and adaptive feedback mechanisms, embedded within inventory and supply models, will facilitate real-time mitigation of disruptions and agile responses to volatility in supply and demand [UT 942][UT 833][UT 869]. Formal multistakeholder engagement, incorporating perspectives from authorities, enterprises, and external actors, should be embedded to foster collaborative governance, particularly at intersections of compliance, safety, and sustainability within supply chain operations [UT 684][UT 876]. Enhanced collaboration between manufacturers and suppliers can be achieved by simulating network effects and digital platform access protocols, promoting integration and shared risk management [UT 609][UT 547]. Additionally, frameworks that allow seamless integration of new decision variables—such as environmental performance and recoverability indices—at early design stages will support proactive, resilient, and sustainable supply chain management [UT 621][UT 707][UT 778][UT 885]. The adoption of fuzzy-logic-augmented system dynamics is recommended to provide robust inventory and resource allocation policies in uncertain environments, thereby minimizing risk while optimizing service levels and cost dimensions [UT 534]. Policy toolkits distilled from simulation results, including pricing guidance or emergency protocols, can further operationalize insights for managers and policymakers [UT 833][UT 609]. Finally, ongoing validation with real operational data, continuously feeding model evolution, is critical to ensuring the relevance, reliability, and sustained uptake of advanced system dynamics tools in diverse supply and manufacturing contexts.
