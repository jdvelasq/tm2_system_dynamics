DEFINITION:

This cluster is defined by the application of system dynamics methodologies to the analysis and management of risk, disaster, and health crises, with particular emphasis on public health threats such as pandemics, epidemics, and disease outbreaks, including the coronavirus (COVID-19). Conceptually, the cluster encompasses the modeling of complex, non-linear interactions among epidemiological, institutional, behavioral, and socio-economic components that drive disease incidence and health outcomes [UT 425][UT 454][UT 141][UT 217][UT 110][UT 112][UT 342][UT 154][UT 400][UT 488][UT 224][UT 286][UT 455][UT 195][UT 235][UT 421][UT 16][UT 64][UT 333][UT 478][UT 221][UT 49][UT 70][UT 423][UT 123][UT 191][UT 352][UT 384][UT 467][UT 348][UT 363][UT 32][UT 177][UT 232][UT 246][UT 239][UT 267][UT 439][UT 211][UT 237]. Methodologically, the cluster is anchored in the use of quantitative, feedback-based simulation platforms such as Vensim, Powersim, and STELLA, integrating tools like causal loop diagrams, stock-and-flow models, and scenario analysis to delineate and test prevention, vaccination, and safety strategies under varied disaster and epidemic conditions. The analytical boundaries extend across system-level risks in healthcare, infrastructure, organizational logistics, disaster preparedness, public safety, and environmental health, with recurring attention to multi-scenario analysis, adaptive risk management, and stakeholder participation. Empirical calibration, dynamic parameter extraction, and multicriteria analyses (e.g., AHP, entropy methods) are leveraged to quantify key factors and policy levers, with operational applications ranging from hospital resource allocation and infectious disease transmission to waste management, pharmaceutical access, psychological impacts, and public health governance. In sum, this thematic family is unified by its systems science approach to elucidating, simulating, and strengthening resilience, prevention, and adaptive response to public health emergencies and broader societal risks.

TRENDS:

Recent literature within this thematic cluster reveals a robust expansion and diversification of system dynamics research, driven by the challenges posed by coronavirus, COVID-19, and broader pandemic and disaster contexts. A clear trend is the widespread adoption of scenario-based simulation and predictive modeling to inform interventions and policy responses under conditions of uncertainty and rapid change [UT 425][UT 454][UT 32][UT 177][UT 267][UT 211]. System dynamics models, including adaptations of classical SIR and Bass Diffusion models, have been instrumental in guiding prevention, vaccination, and disease containment strategies, often through extensive calibration with heterogeneous real-world and governmental data [UT 32][UT 177][UT 232][UT 246][UT 267][UT 439][UT 195][UT 235][UT 221]. The scope of empirical application now extends beyond epidemiology and public health to include the resilience of critical infrastructures (such as hospital logistics, pharmaceutical supply chains, and water systems), urban and organizational safety, and risk assessment in financial sectors [UT 425][UT 342][UT 232][UT 246][UT 267][UT 439][UT 217][UT 110][UT 112]. Methodologically, there is progression toward complex, integrated representations of multi-shock environments—capturing not only disease and health incidence but also economic, social, and psychological impacts (e.g., banking solvency, student crisis behavior, post-COVID-19 sequelae) [UT 154][UT 488][UT 286][UT 400][UT 455][UT 191][UT 352]. The incorporation of participatory approaches, AHP and multicriteria decision analysis, and multi-source data integration has enriched the capacity to simulate feedback structures, risk perceptions, and prevention strategies across diverse public domains [UT 333][UT 141]. Advances in model calibration, iterative sensitivity analyses, and the use of technologies such as blockchain for monitoring preventive behaviors signify a move toward operationally actionable, cross-sectoral insights [UT 237]. Collectively, these trends signal not only the maturation but also the versatility and empirical relevance of system dynamics in contemporary public health, disaster, and risk management research [UT 363][UT 348][UT 478][UT 122][UT 239][UT 195][UT 64][UT 333][UT 211].

CHALLENGES:

System dynamics applications addressing coronavirus, covid-related risks, disasters, pandemics, epidemics, disease incidence, prevention, and public health face a constellation of conceptual and methodological challenges that hinder model development, validation, and implementation. Foremost, the inherent complexity and uncertainty of multifactorial crises complicate the accurate representation of dynamic feedbacks, nonlinear causal pathways, and evolving risk landscapes within infectious disease and disaster contexts [UT 141][UT 246]. Data limitations—ranging from missing or delayed epidemiological statistics, underreported incidence due to weak surveillance or testing, and rapidly changing conditions—undermine model calibration, validity, and real-time adaptability [UT 211][UT 235][UT 425][UT 454]. The integration of heterogeneous system components (epidemiological, psychological, economic, and infrastructural) and interactions across multiple time horizons or organizational levels challenges the construction of parsimonious yet sufficiently comprehensive models [UT 16][UT 191][UT 221][UT 467]. Methodologically, difficulties persist in identifying, weighting, and classifying interacting risk factors, capturing stakeholder preferences and behavioral dynamics, and translating real-world policy levers—such as vaccination, prevention, or resource allocation—into simulation-ready structures under high uncertainty and scenario dependency [UT 110][UT 217][UT 232][UT 267]. Participation-based model building (e.g., GMB), while valuable for contextual relevance, faces obstacles in stakeholder engagement and sustained input [UT 154][UT 400][UT 224][UT 488][UT 455]. Implementation lags further manifest in the translation of model insights into actionable, validated interventions, especially amid shifting crisis parameters and across sectors such as health, safety, and economic resilience [UT 32][UT 195][UT 239][UT 342][UT 417][UT 423][UT 123][UT 70]. Collectively, these enduring limitations underscore the need for more agile data integration, nuanced conceptual frameworks, and enhanced stakeholder interfaces to realize the full potential of system dynamics for pandemic, disaster, and public health risk management.

OPPORTUNITIES:

Future research opportunities in this thematic cluster are marked by the need to expand the conceptual and methodological frontiers of system dynamics, especially in the context of coronavirus, covid, disaster, epidemic, and broader public health challenges. Advancing the granularity and scalability of system dynamics models—through integration of high-frequency, real-time data and multidimensional feedback—remains essential for capturing disease incidence evolution and emergent systemic risks [UT 425][UT 141]. Cross-disciplinary model synthesis, linking epidemiological, socioeconomic, infrastructural, and behavioral subsystems, promises a more holistic understanding of risk and prevention mechanisms in pandemic and disaster scenarios [UT 195][UT 221][UT 239][UT 267][UT 232]. Methodologically, the adoption of hybrid and adaptive modeling approaches—such as incorporating machine learning, uncertainty quantification, automated calibration, and blockchain-enabled monitoring—could improve real-time model responsiveness, parameter estimation, and empirical robustness, especially for rapidly evolving crises or post-COVID mental health forecasting [UT 191][UT 235][UT 237][UT 211]. To strengthen model validation and policy translation, further emphasis on systematic longitudinal studies, scenario-based simulations, and participatory modeling that broadens stakeholder and community engagement is warranted [UT 154][UT 488][UT 400][UT 455][UT 333][UT 16][UT 232][UT 12]. Unresolved gaps include the retrospective quantification of undertesting, explicit incorporation of cognitive and organizational biases, modeling of psycho-social impacts and behavioral risk perception, and the development of dynamic behavioral incentive structures in vaccination and prevention systems [UT 177][UT 478][UT 421][UT 352]. Lastly, improving the interoperability, transparency, and multicriteria evaluation of system dynamics models across public health, infrastructure, and economic domains, as well as expanding scenario diversity to address multiple and cascading hazards, will foster cumulative learning and more contextually adaptive policies in risk, disaster, and health management [UT 246][UT 267][UT 49][UT 467][UT 348][UT 70][UT 423][UT 363][UT 417].

VALUE:

To maximize the practical value of this cluster in the core area of system dynamics—particularly across domains such as coronavirus, covid, pandemic, epidemic, disaster, disease, and public health—multiple complementary strategies are recommended. First, embedding system dynamics models directly into institutional decision-support frameworks and sector-specific management platforms, notably in hospitals, public health authorities, and critical infrastructure, enables more agile scenario-informed resource allocation and adaptive surge responses tailored to evolving risk, incidence, and contagion trends [UT 425][UT 454][UT 195][UT 16][UT 221][UT 154][UT 400][UT 488][UT 455]. Practical value is further increased by fostering systematic stakeholder engagement and participatory modeling throughout the lifecycle—from problem definition and validation to policy translation and monitoring—thus ensuring interventions are both contextually relevant and theoretically robust [UT 49][UT 348][UT 421][UT 221][UT 154]. Integrating multi-stakeholder feedback mechanisms and establishing co-production practices supports ongoing model calibration, enhances operational relevance, and facilitates continuous organizational learning [UT 425][UT 454][UT 141][UT 195][UT 221]. Real-time data assimilation, robust sensitivity analyses, and transparent communication of model assumptions, uncertainties, and limitations are essential for building model credibility, particularly in rapidly evolving public health and disaster contexts [UT 177][UT 246][UT 211][UT 237]. Adopting modular, user-friendly interfaces—capable of supporting proprietary or real-time data—and leveraging cross-domain insights (e.g., from supply chain or financial risk to health system resilience) will improve scalability and usability for policymakers and frontline practitioners [UT 70][UT 123][UT 232][UT 267][UT 423][UT 363][UT 467][UT 348]. Finally, dissemination strategies involving plain-language summaries, targeted policy briefs, comprehensive training, and regulatory support will accelerate the translation of dynamic insights into actionable improvements in preparedness, prevention, vaccination campaigns, and long-term public health resilience [UT 16][UT 333][UT 417][UT 237][UT 154][UT 400][UT 455].
