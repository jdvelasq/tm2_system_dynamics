DEFINITION:

This cluster is defined by the intersection of system dynamics modeling with the management and optimization of complex industrial, supply, and environmental systems, emphasizing supply chains, manufacturing, recycling, waste management, and technological evolution. Studies operationalize system dynamics using platforms such as Vensim, Stella, Powersim, and iThink to construct and simulate feedback-rich models—incorporating causal loop and stock-and-flow diagrams—to capture the temporal evolution of resources, inventory dynamics, production processes, waste and solid waste cycles, recycling mechanisms, and technological innovation across sectors including electric and automotive manufacturing, renewables, and megaprojects [UT 79, UT 214, UT 93, UT 341, UT 2, UT 217, UT 110, UT 14, UT 282, UT 174; UT 167, UT 495, UT 103, UT 146, UT 98, UT 212, UT 80, UT 151, UT 8, UT 371; UT 59, UT 149, UT 171, UT 213, UT 223, UT 285, UT 433, UT 264, UT 492, UT 6; UT 44, UT 247, UT 456, UT 181, UT 286, UT 157, UT 189, UT 421, UT 483, UT 336; UT 340, UT 21, UT 26, UT 325, UT 137, UT 168, UT 114, UT 414; UT 386, UT 499, UT 124, UT 263, UT 107, UT 89, UT 457, UT 490, UT 43, UT 250; UT 343, UT 276, UT 251, UT 127, UT 33, UT 164, UT 332, UT 35, UT 49, UT 126; UT 216, UT 248, UT 309, UT 430, UT 375, UT 162, UT 71, UT 78, UT 158, UT 265; UT 364, UT 441, UT 466, UT 475, UT 228, UT 298, UT 467, UT 427, UT 422, UT 380; UT 346, UT 347, UT 348, UT 363, UT 366, UT 268, UT 108, UT 176]. Methodologically, the cluster employs system dynamics as both a conceptual and quantitative tool, often integrated with techniques such as evolutionary game theory and structural equation modeling, to analyze multi-actor, multi-stage systems and evaluate policy impacts (e.g., subsidies, green regulations), risk factors, technological transitions, and organizational behaviors. The keywords—supply, recycle, waste, evolutionary, environmental, inventory, electric, value, technological, automotive, renewable, solid, manufacturer, supplier, megaproject, manufacturing, safety, production, power, subsidy, information, engineering, raw—reflect the thematic emphasis on modeling complex, adaptive systems subject to environmental and regulatory pressures, with applications targeting sustainability, resilience, safety, and value creation through circular and adaptive industrial practices.

TRENDS:

Recent developments in this thematic family reveal an increasing sophistication and diversification in the application of system dynamics, driven by the integration of evolutionary game theory, advanced simulation tools, and hybrid quantitative methodologies. Studies are increasingly leveraging system dynamics to address sustainability challenges in manufacturing, supply, inventory, and waste management, as well as in technological and environmental policy domains. Key trends include the modeling of complex multi-agent and multi-scalar supply networks, incorporating manufacturers, suppliers, and raw material flows to capture interactions in value creation, waste recycling, and resilience to shocks—such as geopolitical disruptions and the COVID-19 pandemic [UT 213, UT 285, UT 421, UT 59, UT 149, UT 264]. The automotive, electric, and renewable power sectors feature prominently, with scenario-based approaches and platforms like Vensim facilitating granular exploration of causal feedback, temporal evolution, and policy efficacy in domains ranging from battery recycling and green certificate markets to sustainable inventory and construction solid waste strategies [UT 93, UT 214, UT 167, UT 103, UT 495, UT 332]. Methodologically, blending system dynamics with tools such as entropy-weighted TOPSIS, principal component analysis, structural equation modeling, and fuzzy controllers enhances empirical rigor and allows for the integration of operational, behavioral, and societal variables [UT 2, UT 217, UT 158, UT 386]. There is also a discernible shift toward modeling regulatory interventions, environmental subsidies, and incentive schemes, reflecting an emphasis on environmental and safety outcomes in megaprojects, manufacturing, and urban solid waste systems [UT 44, UT 157, UT 336, UT 110]. Across these domains, digitalization, multi-actor collaboration, and real-time scenario analyses are reshaping both the scope and depth of system dynamics research, underscoring its role as a versatile and integrative framework for addressing the evolution of supply, recycle, waste, and technological innovation at the interface of environmental and industrial systems [UT 8, UT 374, UT 146, UT 176, UT 358].

CHALLENGES:

This cluster reveals persistent and multifaceted challenges spanning conceptual, methodological, and implementation domains in the modeling and management of complex supply, manufacturing, recycling, and waste systems. Conceptually, effectively capturing the interconnected, dynamic feedback among supply chains, waste flows, environmental and technological changes, and organizational or stakeholder behaviors remains a core difficulty, especially when economic, regulatory, and social dimensions intertwine and key drivers are not directly observable [UT 336, UT 167, UT 189, UT 103, UT 495, UT 371, UT 250, UT 457, UT 414, UT 309, UT 216]. Methodologically, models often struggle with the accurate identification, calibration, and weighting of critical variables and scenario parameters—such as those influencing evolutionary game dynamics, inventory resilience, technological substitution, and risk propagation—while contending with uncertainties in data quality and external drivers like policy shifts or market disruptions [UT 79, UT 217, UT 151, UT 212, UT 213, UT 492, UT 264, UT 59, UT 44, UT 157, UT 499, UT 386, UT 490, UT 366]. Integrating qualitative dimensions—such as human factors, stakeholder decision-making, informal actor contributions, and bounded rationality—alongside quantitative information remains methodologically taxing, often limiting the robustness and representativeness of simulation outputs [UT 151, UT 98, UT 223, UT 107, UT 21, UT 340]. Implementation-wise, significant obstacles persist in translating simulation insights into actionable strategies or policies, particularly due to data limitations, diverse stakeholder interests, institutional inertia, and region-specific context sensitivity [UT 341, UT 2, UT 282, UT 93, UT 214, UT 475, UT 366, UT 346, UT 358, UT 363, UT 108]. The time lag between interventions and observable impact, limited model generalizability, and marginal effectiveness of policy levers—such as subsidies, landfill fees, or regulatory changes—further complicate strategic alignment and validation efforts [UT 8, UT 103, UT 168, UT 276, UT 181, UT 247, UT 286, UT 456, UT 124, UT 89, UT 422, UT 466]. Ultimately, these challenges underscore the need for rigorous empirical validation, multidisciplinary integration, and adaptive modeling strategies capable of reflecting the evolving realities of supply, recycle, waste, manufacturing, and environmental systems.

OPPORTUNITIES:

In this cluster, significant research opportunities arise from the convergence of methodological advancements, empirical gaps, and emerging thematic domains. There is pronounced scope for integrating system dynamics with complementary approaches—such as evolutionary game theory, agent-based modeling, and value stream mapping—to more comprehensively capture stakeholder strategies, behavioral adaptation, and network effects across heterogeneous manufacturing, supply, and waste management contexts [UT 79, UT 93, UT 2, UT 421, UT 456, UT 347, UT 358]. Future research should prioritize hybrid policy modeling that explicitly examines the synergetic impacts of combined fiscal, regulatory, and technological incentives on recycling rates, renewable energy adoption, and sustainable production, especially given the limitations of isolated interventions demonstrated in waste and megaproject management [UT 44, UT 181, UT 340, UT 495]. Expanding system boundaries to include informal sector participation, platform-driven supply coordination, and cross-layer optimization can further illuminate the dynamic interplay of supply, recycle, and waste flows within evolving circular economy frameworks [UT 107, UT 375, UT 430]. Advances in digitalization—such as leveraging live data streams, IoT-BIM synergies, and cloud platforms—invite improved model validation, real-time scenario analysis, and enhanced operational resilience in the face of disruptions or policy shifts [UT 228, UT 346, UT 348, UT 490, UT 463]. Methodologically, there remains a need to refine models by incorporating heterogeneous agent behaviors, multi-scalar and temporal dynamics, and scenario-based sensitivity analysis, thus strengthening predictive power and policy relevance in domains spanning automotive, electric, and solid waste supply chains [UT 264, UT 6, UT 14, UT 379, UT 212]. Lastly, cross-sectoral comparative studies and collaborative empirical initiatives can bridge current silos, codify best practices, and support the theorization of the co-evolutionary dynamics among technology, regulation, and value creation in environmental systems [UT 286, UT 366, UT 214, UT 364, UT 427].

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this system dynamics cluster, it is essential to ground model development in ongoing, participatory collaboration with stakeholders—including manufacturers, suppliers, policy-makers, and waste managers—ensuring that assumptions and parameters reflect operational realities and evolving technological, environmental, and regulatory landscapes [UT 341][UT 93][UT 346][UT 348][UT 358]. Robust model calibration and validation, leveraging diverse and up-to-date empirical datasets from industry, urban case studies, or sector-specific surveys, will increase the credibility, generalizability, and decision relevance of findings [UT 282][UT 214][UT 14][UT 171][UT 492][UT 285]. Practical impact can be amplified by embedding real-time feedback loops and scenario-based planning tools, facilitating dynamic adaptation in inventory management, waste recycling, electric power transitions, and megaproject resilience—particularly during systemic shocks such as pandemics or climate-induced crises [UT 79][UT 217][UT 347][UT 346][UT 286][UT 336][UT 49][UT 276]. Integrating advanced digital platforms, user-friendly dashboards, and modular decision-support systems will enable continuous monitoring, transparent scenario analysis, and rapid operational adjustment across solid waste, supply, and value chains [UT 347][UT 346][UT 371][UT 430][UT 248][UT 213][UT 223]. Explicitly considering human factors, organizational learning, and behavioral realism within models—such as the roles of overconfidence, risk-sharing, and informal sector dynamics—will further align simulation outputs with real-world production, recycling, and safety management challenges [UT 421][UT 456][UT 107][UT 89][UT 343]. Finally, expanding cross-sector innovation networks, standardizing modeling concepts, and scaling knowledge-sharing platforms will foster wider implementation, promote capacity building, and bridge gaps between theoretical advances and tangible environmental, economic, and social sustainability outcomes [UT 414][UT 463][UT 358][UT 364][UT 422].
