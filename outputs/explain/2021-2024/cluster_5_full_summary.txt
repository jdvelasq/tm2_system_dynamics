DEFINITION:

This cluster is defined by its focus on the intersection of energy systems, economic development, and carbon emissions, as operationalized through system dynamics modeling frameworks. The literature consistently emphasizes the dynamic and intertwined relationships among energy consumption and production, economic activity (including gross domestic product and sectoral growth), and carbon management, capturing their interdependencies through feedback loops, causal diagrams, and iterative simulation. Methodologically, studies utilize quantitative platforms such as Vensim, Stella, and Powersim Studio to construct models that integrate diverse variables—ranging from technology adoption, policy interventions, and regulatory incentives to infrastructure investment, population growth, and shifts toward renewables or efficiency improvements [UT 368][UT 243][UT 174][UT 495][UT 8][UT 86][UT 275][UT 114][UT 43][UT 242][UT 273][UT 377][UT 289][UT 346][UT 427][UT 95][UT 422][UT 441][UT 241][UT 489][UT 234][UT 108]. Empirical applications span micro- and macro-level systems, encompassing sectors like power generation, CCUS retrofitting, hydropower, construction, oil and gas, urban resource management, transport, waste-to-energy, and public services. Scenario and policy analysis are central, with simulation and optimization supporting the assessment of alternative energy, economic, or carbon strategies, as well as sectoral and regional transitions toward low-carbon development. The cluster’s conceptual and methodological scope is ultimately defined by its use of system dynamics to quantitatively analyze, forecast, and strategically inform the impact of energy-related economic activities on carbon emissions and broader environmental outcomes, enabling evidence-based policy development for sustainable, decoupled economic and environmental progress.

TRENDS:

Recent research within this thematic cluster is characterized by a pronounced evolution and diversification in the application of system dynamics to interrogate the intricate interplay among economic growth, energy system transformation, and carbon emission trajectories. The cluster exhibits a marked proliferation of scenario-based simulation studies that move beyond discrete sectoral analyses to more integrated, holistic models, encompassing the water–energy–food nexus, technology transfer, and cross-sectoral linkages like transportation, building, and industrial supply chains [UT 53][UT 275][UT 174][UT 379][UT 433][UT 483]. Analytical sophistication continues to advance through the adoption of multi-loop feedback structures, multi-scenario and sensitivity analyses, and the use of optimization techniques for policy evaluation, frequently grounded in large-scale, high-resolution, or longitudinal data [UT 448][UT 174][UT 86][UT 459][UT 262][UT 43]. Empirical applications prominently address regional and global sustainability agendas, often forecasting over decadal horizons and aligning with carbon neutrality, carbon peaking, and decarbonization strategies [UT 234][UT 8][UT 108]. Notably, the integration of renewable energy technologies—including photovoltaics and green hydrogen—is increasingly central, alongside the emergence of hybrid system dynamics models incorporating ecological and kinetic modeling frameworks to better capture energy conversion and feedback dynamics [UT 45][UT 247][UT 483][UT 284][UT 441]. Studies span diverse geographies (e.g., China, Sri Lanka, Cape Town, Greece, Spain, Lomé, Beijing, Harbin, Liaoning) and policy foci, from carbon quota allocation and low-carbon transport to infrastructure investment and biogas production [UT 243][UT 495][UT 489][UT 377][UT 289]. The field is further distinguished by its shift toward data-driven calibration and validation, deeper sensitivity testing, and increasing emphasis on decoupling gross economic output from carbon-intensive energy systems, underscoring both policy relevance and methodological innovation [UT 95][UT 422][UT 12][UT 143][UT 234].

CHALLENGES:

This thematic cluster is defined by persistent conceptual, methodological, and implementation challenges arising from the inherent complexity of economic-energy-carbon interactions. At the conceptual level, modeling the intricate interdependencies among energy system change, economic growth, and carbon targets requires careful treatment of nonlinearities, feedback loops, and time lags, complexities further exacerbated by sectoral specificity and evolving policy contexts [UT 368][UT 8][UT 459][UT 262]. Methodologically, the integration of disparate temporal and spatial resolutions—such as hourly energy use alongside annual gross economic indicators—often necessitates hybrid or interdisciplinary modeling frameworks but introduces calibration difficulties, magnified by data scarcity and limited reliability of regional time series [UT 174][UT 108][UT 234][UT 448][UT 275][UT 433]. Model parameterization remains a significant obstacle, as the multifaceted effects of policy incentives and technology diffusion—particularly in developing economies or sector-specific cases like photovoltaic adoption or agricultural supply chains—are typically under-quantified and sensitive to stakeholder behavior, information asymmetry, and bounded rationality [UT 247][UT 379][UT 114][UT 441]. Ensuring empirical validity and robustness is hampered by incomplete data, scenario-dependent uncertainty, and inherent challenges in generalizing from local contexts to broader policy prescriptions, particularly where feedbacks are complex or external shocks (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic) induce dynamic adaptation [UT 432][UT 1][UT 49][UT 422][UT 377][UT 244][UT 273][UT 129][UT 346][UT 95][UT 489]. Implementation is further complicated by governance barriers, sectoral siloing, and the misalignment of simulated dynamics with actual stakeholder decision-making, which hinders the practical application of model insights to integrated energy, economic, and carbon governance [UT 108][UT 234][UT 243][UT 495][UT 53][UT 201][UT 157]. Addressing these issues demands not only methodological innovation but also improved data infrastructures and closer alignment between model development and real-world policy processes.

OPPORTUNITIES:

Synthesizing insights from the reviewed literature, this thematic cluster reveals a rich array of research opportunities at the intersection of economic growth, energy transitions, and carbon mitigation. There is wide consensus on the need to extend methodological sophistication in system dynamics models by integrating multi-sectoral and multi-scale linkages across domains such as energy, economy, water, land, and population, with particular attention to indirect effects and trade-offs among economic, energy, and carbon objectives [UT 275][UT 53][UT 461][UT 129]. Advancing cross-sectoral and multi-scalar modeling—including coupling micro-level behavioral, technological, and socio-economic dynamics with macro-level demand projections—can better capture systemic drivers and inhibitors in low-carbon transitions [UT 422][UT 114][UT 273]. Future work should pursue deeper empirical calibration and validation through high-resolution, spatially explicit, and longitudinal data, particularly for rapidly developing or urbanizing regions, to improve model robustness, sensitivity, and generalizability [UT 448][UT 495][UT 242][UT 244][UT 377][UT 346][UT 427]. Further, expanding hybrid approaches—such as embedding agent-based or network models, game-theoretic integration, and advanced data analytics—holds promise for representing micro-level feedbacks, stakeholder-specific behaviors, and nonlinearities inherent in economic and energy systems [UT 441][UT 459][UT 284]. Scenario analysis and policy simulation can be enriched by examining a broader spectrum of intervention strategies, sensitivity to disruptive factors (e.g., climate or regulatory shifts), and the co-design of policy alternatives with diverse stakeholders [UT 86][UT 174][UT 243][UT 368][UT 234][UT 108]. Emerging priorities also include exploring policy synergies, institutional feedbacks, multi-level governance, and comparative transferability studies to inform regionally differentiated strategies for sustainable economic and energy system development [UT 215][UT 45][UT 37][UT 108][UT 441].

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster in the domain of system dynamics, especially at the intersection of economic, energy, and carbon challenges, a suite of synergistic strategies is recommended to bridge the gap between academic models and real-world application. Embedding scenario- and stakeholder-driven processes throughout model development and application—through participatory workshops, iterative feedback, and co-modeling—ensures that models are tailored to concrete institutional, technological, and demographic contexts, thereby increasing both the legitimacy and relevance of resulting policy guidance [UT 368][UT 495][UT 441][UT 379][UT 201][UT 234]. Modularity and interoperability should be prioritized to enable customization for specific sectors (e.g., transportation, agriculture, construction), facilitate knowledge transfer, and support cross-sectoral integration of interventions—key for tackling the gross complexity of energy transitions and carbon mitigation portfolios [UT 215][UT 483][UT 1][UT 346][UT 427]. To boost robustness and scalability, standardized and open-access data protocols, ongoing empirical calibration using granular regional economic and energy datasets, and real-time data integration are essential [UT 377][UT 432][UT 433][UT 95][UT 346]. Equally important is the actionable communication of findings: user-friendly interfaces, visualization tools, and scenario-based narratives should translate simulation results into accessible economic and carbon performance metrics for policymakers at various levels [UT 8][UT 243][UT 234][UT 108][UT 157][UT 201][UT 45]. Flexible, adaptive modeling approaches that can rapidly test the impacts of shocks or new policies, as well as hybrid models integrating behavioral and technological expertise, are vital for maintaining policy relevance amid volatility [UT 275][UT 174][UT 284][UT 49]. Finally, integrating system dynamics outputs with tangible policy levers—such as carbon pricing, technology subsidies, or labor programs—and embedding these tools in participatory planning cycles will help ensure sustainable economic outcomes, effective carbon reduction, and continual strategy optimization as new data and disruptions emerge [UT 114][UT 273][UT 129][UT 43][UT 262][UT 289][UT 489][UT 422].
