DEFINITION:

This thematic cluster is defined by the strategic application of system dynamics modeling to analyze, simulate, and optimize carbon emissions, energy demand, and sustainability practices within industrial, transportation, and especially construction domains. Central to this cluster is the operationalization of dynamic simulation frameworks, notably utilizing platforms such as Vensim, Stella, and Powersim, to evaluate complex feedback loops, causal relationships, and non-linear system behaviors that characterize carbon management challenges across supply chains, prefabricated construction, and contractor operations [UT 214, UT 368, UT 495, UT 3, UT 14, UT 209, UT 188, UT 435, UT 243, UT 194; UT 371, UT 44, UT 45, UT 215, UT 264]. Methodologically, the literature encompasses causal loop diagrams, stock-flow models, scenario analysis, equilibrium strategies, and sensitivity analyses to capture the multifaceted and dynamic interplay among economic drivers (e.g., cost, profits), technological innovation (e.g., green technologies, intelligent and prefabricated construction), policy mechanisms (e.g., emission trading markets, government subsidies), as well as contractor and stakeholder behavior within emission-relevant supply and value chains [UT 271, UT 263, UT 273, UT 310, UT 468]. The conceptual boundaries are further extended through integrative models linking carbon, energy, and material flows with sustainability levers, resource efficiency, and low-carbon competitiveness, underpinned by circular economy principles and international carbon policy objectives [UT 228, UT 427, UT 95, UT 155, UT 346, UT 199; UT 332, UT 248, UT 296, UT 377, UT 354, UT 397, UT 441, UT 289, UT 294]. Empirical studies address national and sectoral targets (notably ‘Carbon peak and Carbon neutrality’), forecasting carbon and emission reduction potentials and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions ranging from contractor participation and supply chain collaboration to risk assessment in forestry carbon sequestration [UT 234, UT 63, UT 349]. Collectively, this cluster delineates a robust, cross-sectoral system dynamics paradigm integrating carbon, emission, energy, supply, contractor, prefabricated and industrial construction, and policy variables to advance sustainable development goals.

TRENDS:

Recent trends in system dynamics research within this thematic family reflect an intensified and diversified focus on modeling carbon emissions, energy management, and supply chain resilience across the construction and associated industrial sectors. A marked shift is evident toward increasingly sophisticated, multi-factorial models that integrate technological, organizational, and policy variables, moving from single-sector analyses to more holistic examinations of global value chains and sectoral interplay [UT 435, UT 495, UT 214, UT 368, UT 14, UT 44, UT 45, UT 215, UT 371, UT 264]. Scenario-based and dynamic simulations—often encompassing sensitivity, policy, and market-oriented analyses such as green certificates and carbon trading—are now widely adopted to evaluate efficiency trade-offs, emission reduction strategies, and the diffusion of intelligent technologies in construction and supply systems [UT 243, UT 209, UT 44, UT 371, UT 45, UT 43]. Empirical applications are broadening considerably, spanning traditional and prefabricated construction, dam building, fisheries, urban and regional electricity use, and low-carbon transportation, underpinned by the use of real-world case studies and diverse regional datasets [UT 271, UT 263, UT 273, UT 310, UT 468, UT 248, UT 289, UT 354, UT 296, UT 377, UT 397, UT 441]. Innovations such as the coupling of life cycle assessment and gradient carbon pricing with system dynamics, incorporation of IoT/BIM data into simulation environments, and linkages with evolutionary game theory highlight methodological advancements aimed at capturing the complexity of energy–economy–environment systems [UT 228, UT 95, UT 155, UT 346, UT 427, UT 199, UT 121]. Collectively, these developments underscore the field’s transition toward integrated, cross-sectoral models capable of forecasting long-term carbon and energy trends, evaluating policy impacts, and generating actionable recommendations that align operational practices with international sustainability imperatives [UT 188, UT 194, UT 234, UT 63, UT 349].

CHALLENGES:

This thematic cluster is characterized by persistent conceptual, methodological, and implementation challenges that constrain the capacity of system dynamics approaches to effectively inform carbon management, emission reduction, and supply optimization across construction, industrial, and energy sectors. Conceptually, researchers struggle to capture the full spectrum of direct and indirect factors influencing carbon emissions and cost performance, as system boundaries are difficult to delineate amid intertwined supply, transport, and construction processes, resulting in risks of oversimplification or exclusion of key policy-driven and behavioral responses [UT 188, UT 214, UT 435, UT 44, UT 264, UT 52, UT 95, UT 228]. Methodologically, the robust quantification and integration of diverse, often nonlinear variables—spanning technological, economic, geochemical, and policy domains—remain unresolved due to incomplete data, considerable uncertainty, and the complexity of feedback mechanisms [UT 371, UT 44, UT 258, UT 310, UT 354, UT 332, UT 346, UT 234, UT 63]. Life-cycle emissions, especially in scenarios involving prefabricated construction or supply chain transformations, highlight that suboptimal material choices or supply bottlenecks can undermine targeted reductions, calling for more rigorous co-optimization and data calibration [UT 188, UT 248]. Practical implementation faces further obstacles: confined stakeholder engagement, market resistance to carbon-labeled and prefabricated products, and information asymmetry reduce the effectiveness of emission reduction measures and green supply initiatives [UT 3, UT 271, UT 441, UT 332]. The translation from simulation to actionable policy is hampered by model validation difficulties, ambiguous assumptions, and insufficient real-world data integration (e.g., from IoT or government sources) [UT 228, UT 155, UT 349]. Furthermore, multi-objective intervention—balancing environmental, economic, and social goals—is complicated by institutional inertia, adaptive lags, and the need for dynamic policy and technology adaptation in evolving regulatory landscapes [UT 243, UT 14, UT 495, UT 296, UT 377]. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for more integrated, transparent, and adaptive methodologies to close persistent gaps between conceptual modeling and impactful policy implementation.

OPPORTUNITIES:

Synthesizing the identified research opportunities within this cluster highlights a multifaceted agenda for advancing system dynamics applications toward carbon emission reduction in supply and construction domains. A prominent direction involves the development of multi-criteria, multi-sectoral, and multi-agent models that comprehensively capture the interplay among technological, behavioral, institutional, and policy changes as well as complex feedback mechanisms, both at micro and macro levels [UT 435, UT 495, UT 243, UT 3, UT 14, UT 228, UT 95, UT 427]. Integrating life cycle assessment, carbon pricing, and circular economy frameworks with system dynamics, while exploiting real-time analytics and scenario-based modeling, could yield more accurate assessments of both direct and systemic impacts in construction, transportation, and energy systems [UT 188, UT 214, UT 155, UT 199, UT 121]. Notably, dynamic supply chain planning incorporating granular data—including market shocks, contractor resource shifts, and delay propagation in large-scale projects—remains underexplored, particularly in prefabricated and industrial construction, international supply chains, and social housing contexts [UT 258, UT 215, UT 332, UT 248, UT 63, UT 234, UT 349]. Further research is needed to optimize mixed or adaptive policy strategies, combining educational, incentive, and regulatory instruments to shift contractor and user behaviors and manage resource consumption and waste [UT 271, UT 263, UT 273, UT 310]. Comparative, cross-regional studies and high-resolution scenario testing—especially as enabled by advancements in BIM and IoT technologies—can improve understanding of emission drivers and management strategies under uncertainty, facilitating more resilient urban carbon management and sustainable development trajectories [UT 371, UT 296, UT 377, UT 289, UT 349]. Ultimately, a concerted emphasis on refining stakeholder modeling, risk coupling analyses, and collaborative supply chains will deepen insights into pathway optimization for emission reduction and energy transition in both established and under-studied domains [UT 397, UT 441, UT 44, UT 264, UT 45, UT 194].

VALUE:

To enhance the practical value of this cluster, the literature consistently underscores the importance of fostering robust engagement between system dynamics model developers and key stakeholders—including policymakers, industry practitioners, construction contractors, and supply chain actors—through participatory modeling and integrated scenario analysis [UT 495, UT 214, UT 368, UT 194, UT 441, UT 332, UT 234, UT 349]. Operationalizing system dynamics–based decision support tools with transparent, modular, and user-friendly interfaces—particularly those that are interoperable with sector platforms such as BIM—would empower practitioners to co-optimize economic and environmental objectives, including carbon emission reduction, supply resilience, and project cost management [UT 214, UT 368, UT 3, UT 63]. Enhancing model validation with real-time and high-frequency empirical data from field experiments, industry case studies, or IoT-enabled monitoring is critical for increasing robustness, credibility, and scenario relevance [UT 188, UT 209, UT 258, UT 215, UT 377, UT 294, UT 228]. Embedding dynamic feedback mechanisms, adaptive policy levers (e.g., emissions trading, gradient pricing), and systematic tracking of intervention impacts will facilitate continuous policy calibration and responsiveness across construction, supply, and energy-intensive sectors [UT 155, UT 346, UT 273, UT 468]. Multi-factorial intervention portfolios, rather than isolated policy instruments, demonstrate greater simulated and real-world effectiveness for influencing supply-demand equilibria, carbon and waste reduction, and resilient supply chain operation [UT 243, UT 44, UT 264]. Prioritizing cross-sectoral knowledge transfer—including lessons from prefabricated and green construction—and linking policy simulations across local and national scales can further enhance scalability, transferability, and adoption of carbon mitigation strategies [UT 271, UT 310, UT 354, UT 397, UT 248]. By embedding system dynamics–based recommendations within iterative policy review processes as well as day-to-day operational protocols, the field can ensure that models remain adaptive, actionable, and effective in delivering measurable, sustainable outcomes amid evolving technological and regulatory contexts [UT 289, UT 296, UT 349].
